What phishing really is

Attackers donot break into computers first. They trick people. A message looks like it’s from your bank, a delivery company, your boss, or a school, and asks you to click a link, open a file, or share a code.

The goal is usually one of three things: steal a login, steal money, or install harmful software. The trick works because it uses feelings: fear, urgency, curiosity, or greed. This is called social engineering.

Common types

Email phishing

Mass emails posing as banks, shops, or services. Fake sender, urgent message, bad link.

Spear phishing

Aimed at one person, using real details such as your name, job, or boss to look believable.

Fake websites

Look-alike login pages with a slightly wrong address. Anything you type goes to the attacker.

Smishing

Phishing by SMS: “Your parcel is held, pay a small fee here.”

Vishing

Phishing by phone call, often a “bank officer” asking for your OTP or PIN.

Fake job offers

Easy pay, no interview, then a request for a fee or your ID documents.

How to stay safe

  • Pause first. Urgency is the biggest warning sign.
  • Check the sender’s address and the link’s real destination before clicking.
  • Type the website address yourself or use your saved bookmark instead of email links.
  • Never share OTPs, PINs, or passwords. Real companies don’t ask for them.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication and use a password manager. It won’t fill in a fake site.
  • Verify through another channel. Call the company using the number on its official site.
  • If you clicked, change the password, tell your bank or IT team, and run a malware scan.
